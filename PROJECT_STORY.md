# Project Story

## About The Project

We built **SIMULACRA**, an economic policy simulator designed around a bigger idea: public policy should not only be studied through static charts, old case studies, and retrospective analysis. It should also be explored as a living system before decisions are made. Our core belief is that people should be able to **learn not only from the past, but also from possible futures**.

That idea was the real inspiration behind the project. In classrooms, policy discussions often stay abstract: a professor explains inflation, labor shocks, subsidies, tariffs, or welfare reforms, and students absorb the theory after the fact. But economic policy is ultimately about human consequences. We wanted to create a tool that makes those consequences visible, immediate, and discussable. Instead of only asking, "What happened historically?", SIMULACRA asks, "What might happen next, who might feel it first, and why?"

We think that shift matters beyond this project. Tools like this could reshape how economics and public policy are taught. A lecture no longer has to stop at memorizing famous crises or comparing textbook models. Students could test a proposal, watch communities respond, see relationships break or strengthen, observe prices and employment shift, and debate not just whether a policy is efficient, but whether it is socially survivable. That changes policy education from passive review into interactive foresight.

We built the project as a full-stack simulation platform. The frontend uses **Next.js** and **Phaser** to render a live pixel-art city, social graph, event feed, and metrics dashboard, turning policy into something you can watch unfold. The backend uses **FastAPI** and **LangGraph** to generate NPCs, maintain social memory, simulate round-by-round decisions, and stream events in real time over WebSockets. We also added support for uploaded context sources so the simulation can respond not just to a policy document, but also to supporting notes and trend data.

One of the biggest things we learned was that building believable AI simulations is not mainly about calling a model once. The real difficulty is coherence. Characters need to remain consistent over time. Events need to stream in the right order. Movement, dialogue, mood, and UI all need to agree with each other. We learned a lot about state synchronization, real-time event architecture, schema design, and how easily multi-agent systems can feel fake if even one layer drifts out of sync.

The hardest challenges were exactly those edge cases of coherence and realism. We faced desynced overlays, drifting chat bubbles, duplicated event streams, mismatched backend/frontend schemas, and NPC behavior that could become generic unless carefully constrained. We also had to solve a deeper design problem: how to let LLM-driven agents feel expressive and surprising while still fitting inside a structured simulation with measurable outcomes. Much of the project became an exercise in building guardrails, normalization layers, and rendering systems that could make the experience feel trustworthy.

What we are most proud of is not just that the system works, but what it suggests. SIMULACRA points toward a future where policy communication is more interactive, public understanding is more intuitive, and economic education can include simulated futures alongside historical evidence. In that sense, the project is not only a simulator. It is a small argument for a new kind of civic and educational tool: one that helps people reason about policy by letting them experience consequences before they become reality.
